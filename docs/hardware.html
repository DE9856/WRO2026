01

Computing Layer

Two controllers, deliberately, not one. Vision math and hardware timing want opposite things from a processor — one wants throughput, the other wants determinism — so we split them across a board built for each.

U1
Raspberry Pi 4 Model B — 8 GB
Vision pipeline · state machine · planner
QTY 1

Why this one

The whole autonomy stack — HSV segmentation, contour tracking, the world model, and the planner state machine — runs here as ordinary Python/OpenCV. That's a throughput problem: frames arrive continuously and each one has a real deadline, so we wanted the fastest general-purpose SBC we could put inside the 300×200×300 mm envelope rather than a microcontroller running a hand-optimized subset of the pipeline.

8 GB specifically because the vision pipeline, world-model memory, and telemetry logging all run concurrently — 4 GB is enough today, but it left no headroom for the lightweight ML layer mentioned in the perception docs without risking swap-induced frame drops mid-round.

Spec

  • CPUQuad-core Cortex-A72 @ 1.8 GHz
  • RAM8 GB LPDDR4
  • Link to co-processorUSB serial, 115200 baud
  • Camera interfaceUSB 2.0 (UVC)
U2
Arduino UNO R3
Real-time I/O co-processor
QTY 1

Why this one

Everything time-critical lives here instead of on the Pi: servo PWM, stepper STEP pulses, three round-robin ultrasonic pings, an I²C IMU read, and a debounced start-button interrupt. None of that can tolerate the Pi's non-deterministic scheduling — a vision-loop hiccup should never turn into a missed step pulse or a late servo update. Putting it on a separate, simple, deterministic MCU means a slow camera frame degrades steering accuracy, not the ability to steer at all.

It also carries a watchdog: if the Pi goes quiet for >500 ms (a USB unplug, a vision-loop crash), the Arduino stops the drive motor itself rather than coasting on the last command forever.

Spec

  • MCUATmega328P @ 16 MHz
  • Flash / SRAM32 KB / 2 KB
  • DrivesServo, stepper, 3× HC-SR04, IMU, start button
  • Fail-safe500 ms comms-loss watchdog
U3
0.96" I²C OLED Display (SSD1306)
Live on-vehicle status — no laptop tether required
QTY 1

Why this one

Mounted on the top plate so the team can read the planner state (WAIT/OPEN_DRIVE/OBS_DRIVE/PARK_SEEK/PARK_EXEC/DONE), lap/section count, live distance and IMU-heading readings, and battery voltage directly off the robot at the practice table or the vehicle-check bench — without opening a laptop or SSH-ing into the Pi. It's a debug/status aid only; nothing in the autonomous logic reads from it, so it has no effect on scoring either way.

Spec

  • BusI²C, address 0x3C — shares the Arduino's A4 (SDA) / A5 (SCL) bus with the MPU-6050
  • Resolution128×64 monochrome
  • Refresh~4 Hz (rate-limited, non-blocking)
Kept off the critical timing path I²C writes to a display are slow relative to the stepper's STEP pulse timing. The display update is throttled to roughly 4 times a second and issued between round-robin sensor reads rather than every loop() tick, so it can't turn into the kind of blocking call the rest of this firmware was rewritten to avoid.
02

Drive & Actuation

WRO §11.4/§11.5/§11.13 rule out the easy answers — no differential drive, no caster or omni wheels, no electronically-linked "virtual" axle. What's left is one motor, one mechanically-linked driven axle, and a separate steering actuator. Everything below exists to make that constraint verifiable by looking at the wiring, not by trusting the firmware.

M2 · D1
Shinano Kenshi STH-39D219 (NEMA14 stepper) + A4988/DRV8825 driver
Sole drive motor — rear axle
QTY 1 + 1

Why this one

This is the single most-revised decision on the vehicle. The build started with a brushed DC gearmotor through a single-channel L298N — the conventional choice. We switched to a stepper because a stepper's speed control is open-loop and drift-free: a step-pulse frequency maps directly and repeatably to shaft RPM. A DC motor's PWM duty cycle only approximates a given speed, and that approximation shifts with load and with battery voltage as the pack discharges over a 3-minute round — exactly the kind of silent drift that turns into a bad lap-timing or cornering estimate hours after it was calibrated.

Wiring only STEP, DIR, and ENABLE to the driver (the motor's two center-tap wires are left disconnected — bipolar drive) is itself a compliance choice: with no second driver or motor channel present on the harness at all, a firmware bug literally cannot drive a second axis independently, because there's nothing there to drive. A judge inspecting the wiring during the vehicle check can see single-motor compliance directly, without reading the code.

We considered a ULN2003 unipolar driver first, but preferred STEP/DIR's single pulse-train timing model over 4-phase sequencing, and the higher torque bipolar drive makes available.

Spec

  • Step angle1.8° (200 full steps/rev)
  • Wiring6-wire, run bipolar
  • Rated (nameplate)3.5 V DC, 0.7 A/phase
  • DriverA4988 / DRV8825, full-step (MS1–3 default)
  • Arduino pinsD4 DIR · D5 STEP · D10 EN
Trade-off accepted Lower peak torque per gram than the DC gearmotor it replaced, and holding torque draws current even at a standstill unless the driver is explicitly disabled between commands — which vehicle_controller.ino::stopMotor() now does.
Pending verification The 0.35 V driver current-limit (Vref) is calculated from the motor's nameplate rating and an assumed 0.1 Ω sense resistor on the DRV8825 breakout — a convention for that board family, not yet confirmed by lifting a resistor leg. First power-on is done watching the motor closely rather than left unattended.
—
20T → 60T Timing-Belt Pulley Pair
3:1 reduction, motor shaft → rear axle
1 SET

Why this one

A bare NEMA14 shaft has modest holding torque next to the DC gearmotor it replaced. The belt reduction recovers roughly 3× torque at the axle (before belt/bearing losses) in exchange for 3× lower axle RPM at a given step rate — the same trade a DC gearmotor's internal gearbox makes, just external and re-pulleyable without touching the motor or firmware. Both rear wheels are mechanically forced to turn together off this one belt-driven axle, so there's no possibility of the two rear wheels spinning at different rates — which is exactly the "electronic differential" WRO §11.5 disqualifies.

Spec

  • Belt width6 mm
  • Belt length202 mm
  • Ratio3:1 (20T motor / 60T axle)
  • Conversionaxle_rpm = (step_hz / 200) × 60 / 3
M1
MG996R Servo Motor
Ackermann front steering
QTY 1

Why this one

Cheap, widely stocked, and delivers enough torque to drive the Ackermann linkage directly off a single horn without a secondary gear stage. It runs off its own regulated 6 V rail rather than sharing the logic supply — see Power System for why that separation exists.

Spec

  • Control50 Hz PWM, Arduino D9
  • Operating range4.8–7.2 V
  • Steering range±25°
W1–W4
Wheels — 2 driven, 2 steered
Rear: linked to belt axle · Front: free-rotating, Ackermann-steered
QTY 4

Why this configuration

Standard rubber wheels only — no caster, ball, or omni wheel anywhere on the vehicle. WRO §11.4 rules those out outright, and they'd also fight the Ackermann geometry the steering math assumes.

03

Sensors

Each sensor was added to close a specific gap the others couldn't — not as a generic "more data is better" checklist. Where a sensor is redundant to the camera on purpose, that's noted explicitly, because a silently-duplicated signal is a different design decision than a deliberately independent backup.

S1
Logitech C270 — USB Webcam
Primary lane / wall / pillar / parking-marker vision
QTY 1

Why this one

Originally speced as a Raspberry Pi Camera Module V2. We switched mid-project to a plain USB UVC webcam specifically so camera/capture.py reads frames through standard OpenCV VideoCapture instead of Pi-specific picamera2 calls — decoupling the vision pipeline from one particular board's camera API. Mounted 15–20 cm off the ground at a 15° downward tilt, fixed-focus (no autofocus hunting mid-frame).

Spec

  • Resolution640×480 @ 30 fps (MJPG)
  • InterfaceUSB 2.0
  • Mount15–20 cm height, 15° downward tilt
Cost of the switch The C270's horizontal FOV differs from the originally-planned Pi Camera and hasn't been measured yet — camera/angle.py::CAMERA_FOV is a placeholder until it's calibrated against the real unit.
S2–S4
HC-SR04 Ultrasonic — Front / Left / Right
Wall proximity, parking-collision guard, corridor fallback data
QTY 3

Why three, not one

The original design speced a single forward-facing sensor. We added left and right units deliberately: one forward sensor can't detect lateral wall proximity while cornering in the Open Challenge, and can't see a parking marker the vehicle drifts into sideways while still pixel-centered on it. Front feeds the parking-collision guard directly; left/right are read, logged, and shown on the HUD today, with corridor-centering fusion intentionally deferred — see the note below.

Spec

  • Range2–400 cm, ±3 mm
  • Front Trig/EchoD12 / D13
  • Left Trig/EchoD6 / D7
  • Right Trig/EchoD8 / D11
  • PollingRound-robin — one loop() never blocks on >1
Trade-off accepted Round-robin polling from one Arduino means each individual sensor refreshes roughly every 3× the loop interval, not every loop — coverage traded for throughput.
Deliberately unwired, not forgotten Left/right readings aren't yet fused into active corridor-centering steering. Blending an unverified distance threshold into steering that hasn't been checked against a real corridor risks steering confidently into a wall — so the fusion is deferred until it can be calibrated on the physical field, not guessed at from a laptop.
S5
MPU-6050 — IMU (Gyro + Accel)
Yaw heading during parking
QTY 1

Why this one

Added for one specific gap: pixel-centering on the parking markers can tell the vehicle it's centered, but not whether it's parallel to the wall. WRO's own parking rubric (§1.8.2/§10) gives 15/15 for parallel-and-centered versus only 7/15 for centered-but-not-parallel — a camera-only controller structurally cannot chase that other 8 points, because it has no signal for heading error at all. Gyro-Z is integrated (bare I²C register access, no external library) into a running yaw estimate that control/parking_maneuver.py uses to correct steering while reversing into the lot.

Spec

  • BusI²C — A4 (SDA) / A5 (SCL)
  • Gyro range±250 °/s (default)
  • IntegrationGyro-only — drifts over long runs
S6
IR Line Sensor (Analog)
Backup lap / corner-boundary line detection
QTY 1

Why this one

Deliberately redundant to the camera's own orange/blue corner-line detection (camera/lines.py), in case the vision pipeline misses a fast line transition at speed. The uplink event is received and logged, but kept as an advisory signal rather than wired in as authoritative over the camera — a design choice, not an oversight.

Spec

  • OutputAnalog, adjustable sensitivity
  • Arduino pinA0
04

Power System

Two batteries, three rails, and every split between them exists to stop one subsystem's current draw from starving another's voltage at exactly the wrong moment — a brownout mid-turn, or a driver reset mid-lap.

BT1
7.4 V (2S) LiPo
Feeds both step-down regulators
QTY 1

Why this one

Sized to comfortably feed the two logic/servo step-down converters below without sagging under their combined draw. It does not feed the stepper driver directly — see BT2, and why that split matters.

Spec

  • Nominal7.4 V, 2S
  • Capacity≥ 2000 mAh recommended
BT2
14.8 V (4S) Li-ion — Two 2S Packs in Series
Dedicated stepper-driver VMOT supply
QTY 2 (paired)

Why this exists at all

The DRV8825's motor-supply spec floors at 8.2 V. A 7.4 V nominal 2S LiPo sags well below that for a meaningful fraction of a 3-minute round as it discharges — close enough to the driver's floor to risk a brownout reset on the stepper mid-run. Rather than push the single 2S pack closer to its limit, the driver's VMOT is fed from a separate 4S pack (two 2S sub-packs wired in series, ~12 V even at a typical 3.0 V/cell cutoff) — comfortably clear of the 8.2 V floor across the whole discharge curve, with headroom under the 45 V ceiling too.

Spec

  • Nominal14.8 V (2× 2S in series)
  • Full charge~16.8 V
  • At 3.0 V/cell cutoff~12 V
Handle with care The two 2S sub-packs must always be charged separately through their own balance leads — never charge the series-combined string through one port, since neither pack's BMS knows the other exists. Their independent BMS units also don't communicate, so cell balance should be checked before each run rather than assumed. A fuse or PTC on the pack's positive lead is planned, since nothing else in this path limits fault current at 14.8 V.
Not yet validated This is a paper decision — the 4S pack hasn't been assembled or load-tested yet.
REG1
Step-Down Converter — 5 V, ≥ 3 A
Logic rail: Raspberry Pi + Arduino
QTY 1

Why ≥3 A specifically

The Pi 4B under heavy CPU/vision load can pull enough current that an undersized regulator brownouts it mid-frame — the ≥3 A floor exists to keep that headroom under worst-case pipeline load, not just idle draw.

Spec

  • Output5 V regulated
  • Min. current3 A
REG2
Step-Down Converter — 6 V, ≥ 2 A
Dedicated servo rail
QTY 1

Why it's separate from the logic rail

This is the real reason two regulators exist instead of one. A shared logic/servo rail lets a hard steering transient — the servo drawing a current spike mid-turn — sag the voltage feeding the Raspberry Pi. On a vision pipeline, that brief brownout is a full frame-processing stall at exactly the moment the vehicle is turning: the worst possible time to lose steering input. Splitting the rails trades one extra regulator (cost, weight, wiring) for removing that failure mode entirely.

Spec

  • Output6 V regulated
  • Min. current2 A
SW1 · SW2
Rocker Power Switch + Momentary Push Button
Main power ON · start round
QTY 1 + 1

Why exactly these two, and no more

WRO §9.9–§9.11 permit exactly one power switch and one start-button interaction during a round — no physical mode selector, no extra switches. That's why challenge type (--challenge OPEN/OBSTACLE) is a CLI flag decided before power-on, not a physical switch on the chassis: adding one would itself be a rules violation, however convenient it would be on competition day.

WRO §9.9 — no extra interactions WRO §9.10/§9.11 — one switch, one button
BT1 · 2S 7.4 V LiPo → REG1 5 V → Pi 4B + Arduino (logic)
spacer → REG2 6 V → MG996R servo (isolated rail)
BT2 · 4S 14.8 V Li-ion → A4988/DRV8825 VMOT → STH-39D219 stepper

Structure

The one category still mid-build — noted honestly rather than glossed over.

—
Custom 3D-Printed Chassis
Base structure — camera mount, sensor placement, envelope compliance
QTY 1

Why custom instead of an off-the-shelf RC platform

A custom chassis gives exact control over camera-mount geometry and sensor placement, and lets every dimension be checked against the 300×200×300 mm / 1.5 kg envelope (WRO §11.1/§11.2) directly, instead of adapting a stock platform's fixed geometry to fit.

Status

  • Design filesIn cad/, final-intent
  • Physical printIn fabrication, not yet complete
  • Interim dataPrior prototype chassis, until re-measured
Disclosed, not hidden Any weight/dimension figures elsewhere in this repo describe the prior interim prototype used to validate the software pipeline, not the final competition chassis, unless stated otherwise. Full reasoning and timeline: ENGINEERING_JOURNAL.md → Criterion 1.

The rule this page follows

A part earns a spot on this vehicle by solving one specific, named problem — not by being the default or the first thing that worked. Where a part replaced an earlier one (the stepper over the DC gearmotor, the C270 over the Pi Camera), that revision history is kept rather than quietly overwritten, because why we changed our mind is part of the engineering record too.

For the fuller design-reasoning narrative — testing evidence, risk awareness, what we deliberately left unfinished and why — see ENGINEERING_JOURNAL.md. For build order, pin-out, and step-by-step assembly, see the main build guide.
